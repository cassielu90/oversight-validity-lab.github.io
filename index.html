Independent Non-Profit / Empirical AI Oversight Measurement / Target Launch March 2027

Measuring whether human and automated oversight keeps pace with frontier AI.

Frontier safety plans rely on three steps holding together: an agent stays within its authority, a monitor flags suspicious actions, and a person catches what the monitor misses. Labs now report how much agent activity passes through a monitor—not how much serious misconduct that monitor-plus-human system actually misses, or how miss rates shift from testing to deployment.

Oversight Validity Lab (OVL) runs the controlled experiments that answer that question and publishes the results alongside an open, Inspect-compatible harness so permissions, review policies, and release decisions can rest on measured miss rates instead of coverage statistics.

Cross-run coordination and production compromise

Agents in a cybersecurity evaluation with reduced safeguards communicated across runs meant to be isolated via an unsanctioned board, cheated the grader, and compromised Hugging Face production systems. GAP 01

Test conditions are recognisable

When models distinguish evaluation transcripts from deployment at 0.89 AUROC, clean behaviour in benchmark sandboxes cannot be assumed to hold under continuous operation.

GAP 02

Coverage is not sensitivity

Routing nearly all internal agent traffic through a monitor says nothing about how often that monitor, paired with human reviewers, misses serious misconduct.

GAP 03

The human reviewer is part of the system

Models tend to agree with the people they work with, and a reviewer who inspects only monitor-flagged trajectories cannot catch what the monitor failed to surface.

As autonomous agents take on more AI research and engineering, missed oversight failures could quietly weaken the safety work meant to keep future systems under human control.

Targeted Mitigations

Scoring interventions at consequential decision points

Anthropic’s analysis of the AISI incident showed that intervening in a model’s pre-attack reasoning reduced attack rates from 83% to 13%, indicating that intervention timing matters decisively.

OVL evaluates practical mitigations positioned at those exact decision points—including structured authority summaries before privilege-changing calls and reviewer prompts requiring independent evidence before approval—scoring each on detection gained, false alarms added, and reviewer time consumed.

Second Study · Mechanism Evidence

Incident-inspired deployment reconstructions

Beginning with the public 2026 UK AISI / Mythos 5 disclosures, OVL rebuilds the documented deployment conditions as faithfully as public records allow while explicitly cataloguing every departure from the original setup.

We test whether the documented failure mechanism recurs and whether our oversight arrangements intercept it. Findings are reported strictly as reproducible mechanism evidence—never as causal claims about the original incident.

03 · Impact

Theory of Change

Audience 01

Frontier labs & embedded evaluators

What changes Restricted, evidence-scoped claims replace unqualified statements in system cards; review policies and monitoring cadence are calibrated to measured miss rates.
Path to adoption Oversight Validity Reports and the open harness distributed via external evaluation teams and the AI Evaluator Forum.
Audience 02

Organisations deploying agents

What changes Permission configurations that reproduce incident mechanisms are restricted; scarce human review is placed precisely where it demonstrably catches violations.
Path to adoption Direct Year-1 decision pilot, followed by reusable reporting templates for internal risk and engineering leads.
Audience 03

Regulators & standards bodies

What changes Incident reporting and conformity assessment frameworks (UK AISI, EU AI Office, future US incident boards) ask for deployment-side oversight evidence.
Path to adoption A standardised reporting template offered as a draft standard, backed by a harness running natively on UK AISI’s Inspect framework.
