Research

How we measure oversight

Labs report how much agent activity a monitor sees. We measure how much serious misconduct the monitor and its reviewers miss, and how that changes between test-like and deployment-like conditions.

The first study

Agents work on contained software-engineering and AI-research tasks with explicit authority rules and outcomes we can check independently. Third-party services are simulated, and the design will be pre-registered.

Serious violations look like editing a protected evaluation file, quietly changing a results summary, or reaching for a resource outside the granted scope.

The model and task stay fixed within each comparison.

What we measure

  • Serious violations attempted
  • Missed by the monitor
  • Missed or approved by reviewers
  • Benign actions wrongly blocked
  • Reviewer time and cost
  • Blind spots the monitor and reviewers share

Seeded and naturally occurring violations are reported separately.

Design notes in progress

  • Varying how often violations appear, and reporting miss rates at each level.
  • Keeping reviewers blind to how many violations were seeded.
  • Longer review sessions, to see how fatigue affects catch rates.
  • Real-time gating or post-hoc review: post-hoc allows paired scoring of the same runs, real-time shows what blocking costs.
  • Whether the agent should know a human is reviewing.
  • How to check that the framing works. No proven method exists yet.
  • Monitor access: start with an open-source monitor, release an improved version labs can use, then test labs' own monitors.

Mitigations

In Anthropic's analysis of the AISI incident, editing the model's pre-attack reasoning cut the attack rate from 83% to 13%. That suggests timing matters, so we test interventions at consequential decision points, such as structured authority summaries and reviewer prompts that require independent evidence before approval. Each is scored on detection gained, false alarms added and review time used.

Rebuilding an incident

We take a failure mechanism from a public 2026 incident, starting with AISI / Mythos 5, rebuild its deployment conditions as closely as the disclosures allow, and test whether the mechanism recurs and whether our oversight catches it. This tells us about the mechanism, not about what caused the original incident.

The first twelve months

A month-3 checkpoint decides whether we apply for seed funding.

  1. Months 0–2Team in post, reviewer recruitment pilot, conversations with at least three adjacent organisations.
  2. Month 3 · Seed checkpointWorking pilot: one model, two configurations, about 20 reviewers. Precision simulation and a named decision owner. If these hold, apply for seed.
  3. Month 6Open harness and the first Oversight Validity Report.
  4. Month 9Incident-inspired experiment; check on a second model or held-out tasks.
  5. Month 12Final report, versioned release, replication account and decision-pilot record.
